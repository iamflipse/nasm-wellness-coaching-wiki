NASM Wellness Coaching — Wiki

Personal study notes on the National Academy of Sports Medicine (NASM) video course Principles of Wellness Coaching: 73 lessons, condensed into 24 concept pages.

What the course is

The course trains wellness coaches. NASM defines wellness coaching as the active process of applying learned and developed strategies that lead to the mental, emotional and physical betterment of life. Underneath every strategy is one thing: the choices a client makes. The coach does not tell clients what to do. The coach helps them see what is influencing their health, choose their own next steps, and build habits they can eventually keep up without the coach.

Two words come up again and again in the course:

  • Balance: stability between stressors and the ability to cope, across all areas of wellness.
  • Consistency: small adjustments to behaviour that add up to significant long-term change.

Wellness Coaching and the Six Dimensions (OPSISE)

Lessons covered: 1 (Welcome), 3 (Dimensions of Wellness)

Key ideas

  • NASM's definition: wellness coaching is the active process of applying learned and developed strategies that lead to the mental, emotional and physical betterment of life.
  • It all comes down to choices. The coach doesn't dictate. The coach supports clients in making their own choices and finding their own best practices.
  • Not perfection. The goal is not to be perfect in any one area. The areas of wellness work together to improve stress levels, health and peace of mind.
  • The end goal is independence. The coach guides, motivates and encourages so that clients form habits they can eventually keep up on their own (Lesson 1).
  • Mind–body link. Most body systems (gut, immune system, heart, muscles, even bones) communicate in both directions with the brain, so brain wellness is part of whole-body wellness (Lesson 1).

Balance and consistency

TermMeaning in the course
BalanceHelping clients find stability between their stressors and their ability to cope, and equilibrium across the areas of wellness
ConsistencyMaking small adjustments to behaviour that add up to significant long-term change

The six dimensions of wellness (OPSISE)

LetterDimensionWhat it covers
OOccupationalA client's work and their attitude towards it. Contentment depends on the career fitting their interests, beliefs and values.
PPhysicalNutrition, hydration and physical activity. Regular movement is linked with higher levels in other dimensions, such as mental and emotional wellness.
SSocialHealthy relationships, which can lead to a healthier lifestyle. Interaction is described as a necessity for human existence.
IIntellectualStimulating the mind. People who challenge their beliefs, think creatively, solve problems and plan report less stress and conflict.
SSpiritualAligning actions with values and beliefs; questioning one's own existence and being tolerant of other beliefs.
EEmotionalIdentifying and accepting feelings. Clients who express feelings freely build deeper relationships and handle conflict with more clarity and maturity.

The coach's scope

Occupational, spiritual and intellectual wellness matter, but the course says they fall outside a wellness coach's influence. An NASM wellness coach focuses on physical and mental/emotional wellness through four focus areas:

  1. Movement
  2. Mental and emotional wellbeing
  3. Nutrition
  4. Recovery and regeneration

(The welcome lesson lists the course's five areas as these four plus coaching itself.)

Ambivalence and small steps

  • Ambivalence means having conflicting desires. Coaches often have to help clients through it.
  • In moments of ambivalence, remind clients that small changes can produce lasting results.
  • When motivated, people tend to do too much too soon: throwing out every treat in the house, or running three miles after a year of nothing more than short walks. These surges rarely last and can knock other areas, such as mental wellbeing and recovery, off track.
  • Celebrate micro-victories. Small wins create upward spirals and positive feedback loops that strengthen autonomy, meaning and purpose, and connection with others.
  • Motivational interviewing helps here: listen without judgement or correction to understand the client's perspective and meet them where they are.

Coaching in practice

  • "Which area of your wellness feels most important to you right now?"
  • "What's one small, consistent change you could make this week?"
  • "Part of you wants this change and part of you doesn't. Tell me about both sides."
  • "You did that three days in a row. How does it feel to notice that?"
  • When a client wants to overhaul everything at once: "That's a lot of energy. What's the smallest piece of this we could start with so it lasts?"

Quotes

"The choices we make are central to our wellness." (Lesson 3)

"Our instinct is often to do too much too soon when we feel motivated." (Lesson 3)

Growth Mindset

Lessons covered: 2 (Growth Mindset); also draws on 24 (Performance & Cognition) and 60 (Coaching Along the Continuum)

Key ideas

  • A growth mindset is the belief that abilities can grow with effort, learning and practice. Lesson 60 puts it as the idea that intelligence can be nurtured with learning and effort.
  • A fixed mindset tends to resist learning opportunities. A growth mindset tends to embrace them (Lesson 24).
  • Mindsets are not static. Coaches can help clients move from fixed to growth through persistence, effort and practice. A growth mindset improves attitudes to learning, builds resilience and can improve personal outcomes (Lesson 24).
  • Growth mindset is part of cognitive health. It shows up in self-attributions such as "I can do this", "I'm working hard", "I'm making progress" (Lesson 24).
  • Clients with high self-efficacy usually already have a growth mindset and intrinsic motivation. Coaching them is about expanding that mindset. Sometimes it also means reminding them to stay realistic (Lesson 60).

How to cultivate a growth mindset (Lesson 2)

StrategyWhat it means
Look for inspirationSeeing what is possible for others reminds you what is possible for you
Set meaningful goalsScience suggests growth mindset helps most when goals matter to the person
Seek supportFind instructors, coaches, mentors and peers who share your goals and can give information and feedback
Celebrate and document progressRecording progress and sharing it with others creates an upward spiral of confidence for the next goal
Look backwardRecall things you learned or did that weren't easy. Past growth gives confidence about future growth

The lesson stresses that growth is not a do-it-yourself project. Building community and support is part of the plan.

Movement as growth-mindset training

Lesson 53 adds that movement milestones are a direct way to train a growth mindset: you take on a challenge, ask for and accept support, put in effort, make progress and finally master something. That concrete evidence can carry over to other life goals. See Movement Motivation.

Coaching in practice

  • "Think back to something you learned that wasn't easy at first. How did you get through it?"
  • "Who could support you with this goal: a friend, a class, a group?"
  • "How would you like to keep track of your progress so you can see it?"
  • "What would it look like to treat this as something you're learning, not something you're either good or bad at?"
  • With a high-efficacy client: "I love your ambition. What's a realistic first version of this?"

Quotes

"Having a growth mindset and doing amazing things is really not a do-it-yourself project." (Lesson 2)

"Sometimes looking backward is actually one of the best ways to get that confidence about moving forward." (Lesson 2)

The Coach's Role, Evidence and the Coaching Relationship

Lessons covered: 4 (Coach's Primary Responsibility), 5 (Evidence-Based Practice), 6 (Coaching Relationships), 47–48 (The Power of Helping One Person), 49 (Effective Coaching)

The coach's primary responsibility (Lesson 4)

  • The coach's main job is to increase the client's awareness of what is influencing their health and life. That awareness helps them start thinking about change.
  • Whole-person health: it is not just diet, exercise or stress. Stress management, sleep hygiene, food choices and exercise are intertwined. Consistent choices across them create an upward spiral towards vitality, energy and resilience.
  • The aim is for clients to experience health, not just think about it. The lesson compares it to seeing a flower in bloom: you feel its beauty without having to name it.
  • Meeting clients where they are comes from curiosity. One expert calls it "imaginative empathy": a genuine wish to know what it is like to be this person.
  • Trust small steps. Ask for the smallest choice the person could make today that fits their goal, even if they can't yet see how it gets them there.

Evidence-based practice (Lesson 5)

  • Check the science behind claims. Build critical-thinking and research skills.
  • Peer-reviewed journals are the gold standard: other scientists review a paper, then accept it, ask for revisions or reject it.
  • Combine evidence with empathy to find the right balance for each person.
  • Work with humility and honesty, in partnership, helping clients find answers and workable solutions.

The coaching relationship (Lesson 6)

The relationship is described as the foundation of coaching success and the essential factor in outcomes. It is your responsibility to build trust and rapport.

FeatureWhat it looks like
The coaching containerA safe space where the client feels seen, heard and respected, and where action and change can happen
Client-centred and client-drivenThe client defines success. The coach never dictates how or what
Co-createdThe agenda, the plan and even each session emerge from collaboration, not top-down direction
Process and outcomeThe tools, mindset shifts and practices gained along the way matter as much as the end goal; the client keeps them for future goals
PresenceCuriosity, listening (verbal and non-verbal), witnessing, thoughtful questions and reflection
Not fixingThe coach is there to listen, not to fix the client

"It's all about you and it's not at all about you." This means being aware of yourself and of the client. Self-awareness stops your own projections, story or reactions from tripping you up. It is never the client's job to hold space for your emotional process.

Effective coaching (Lesson 49)

  • Rapport and communication build trust. Consistency and self-awareness are also key.
  • Intuition: trust yourself to choose the right questions, and let the client guide the conversation.
  • Empathy means understanding and sharing another person's feelings. It is not sympathy (feeling sorry for someone).
  • Active listening: take in what the client says fully, then ask permission before sharing what you know.
  • Reflective listening: paraphrase their words back to them. This brings clarity and shows that what they share matters.
  • Listen beyond words, use open-ended questions, and let silence feel comforting and safe.

The power of helping one person (Lessons 47–48)

These are short reflections. You never know how helping the one person in front of you might ripple out to many others. Lesson 48 gives an example of a client who came back after four weeks of making the agreed changes, feeling and moving better at home and at work.

Coaching in practice

  • "What's the smallest choice you could make today that fits this goal?"
  • "Can I share what I know about this, or would you be open to hearing what has worked for others?"
  • "What I hear you saying is… Did I get that right?"
  • "If I understand you correctly, you're experiencing…"
  • "What would you like to focus on in our time today?"
  • Allow a pause after a big statement instead of filling the silence.

Quotes

"As the coach you're not there to fix your client. You're really there to listen to them." (Lesson 6)

"It's all about you and it's not at all about you." (Lesson 6)

"Ultimately, you want the client to guide the conversation, not the other way around." (Lesson 49)

Locus of Control and Attributional Retraining

Lessons covered: 7 (Locus of Control), 8 (Attribution Retraining / Blame)

Locus of control (Lesson 7)

Locus of control is how much control a person feels they have over parts of their life, such as their health and wellness. It affects behaviour, confidence and consistency.

Internal locus of controlExternal locus of control
Belief"I have direct control over what happens to me""My fate is up to others or to chance"
SkillsBelieves they have the knowledge and skills to overcome challengesDoesn't believe they can change the situation
Typical responseTakes ownershipBlames outside sources and feels helpless
Health linkDescribed as a good thing for wellnessLinked with higher stress and more illness

What shapes it

The course names three factors:

  1. How much control the person perceives they have
  2. Their ability to adapt and modify behaviours
  3. Their capacity to predict outcomes from specific behaviours

Building an internal locus

  • Awareness and education are key.
  • Help clients identify the behaviours they can control, and build a plan around their strengths.
  • Reprioritise behaviours so they support the client's goals.
  • Learn how and when to modify behaviours. When target behaviours change, long-term goals may need to change too.
  • Help clients predict likely outcomes of their behaviours.
  • Autonomy and empowerment along the way increase their sense of control, and lead towards self-efficacy.

Attribution and attributional retraining (Lesson 8)

Clients will often say things are getting in the way, and the lesson says these are almost always external: time, work, family. Attribution theory asks what is getting in the way (what the person blames). Attributional retraining asks how can I retrain my thoughts, concepts and perceptions to get past it.

Three questions about an attribution

QuestionOptions
Where is it?Internal or external
Can it be controlled?Controllable or uncontrollable
Will it last?Stable or unstable

The worst place for a client is believing the obstacle is uncontrollable and will never change.

How to retrain

  • Go small. Big change needs a lot of motivation, and nobody is highly motivated all the time. Pick a step small enough that it still works when motivation is low.
  • It doesn't have to be challenging. It has to be something the client can actually do.
  • Celebrate it, then do it again.
  • Drop the all-or-nothing mentality. Do a little bit and let behaviour change build along the way.
  • Two ingredients: the right strategy, and actual effort (even at small levels).
  • Small strategies let clients get around blame and guilt, and build confidence bit by bit until they can move forward on their own.

Coaching in practice

  • "What gets in the way most often?" Then explore it: "Is that something within your control, partly in your control, or outside it?"
  • "Is that always true, or are there times it's different?" (tests whether the obstacle is stable)
  • "Given that your schedule is packed, what's a version of this so small you could do it even on your worst day?"
  • "Which parts of your day do you feel most in charge of?"
  • "If you did X, what do you predict would happen?"
  • "You did it. Let's take a moment to celebrate that."

Quotes

"Big change takes a lot of motivation, and you're not going to be a lot motivated all the time." (Lesson 8)

"This isn't about all or nothing. It's about doing a little bit." (Lesson 8)

Negativity Bias, Self-Compassion and Shame

Lessons covered: 9 (Negativity Bias / Cutting Yourself Slack), 54 (Addressing Shame with Empathy), 55 (Addressing Shame Q&A)

Negativity bias (Lesson 9)

  • All humans struggle and suffer. Breaking the cycle of beating yourself up is what the lesson calls "cutting yourself some slack".
  • Negativity bias is a tendency to focus more on the challenging or negative things around us than on the positive ones.
  • It helped our ancestors survive, because watching for threats kept them and their dependants alive.
  • Today, with basic needs mostly met, it can mean dwelling on everything that is wrong. That predisposes people to anxiety, depression and negative self-talk.
  • Knowing the bias exists lets us get ahead of it by deliberately focusing on the positives in ourselves and the world.

(The bias also appears in Stress and Social Wellness, where the course describes us as an ancient brain in a modern environment.)

Self-compassion: three parts (Lesson 9)

PartWhat it isIts opposite
Common humanityRecognising that everyone suffers and struggles, and that you deserve support and care like anyone elseIsolating, withdrawing, telling yourself you're broken
Self-kindnessBeing warm, patient and comforting about your own shortcomingsIgnoring, belittling or criticising yourself
MindfulnessExamining your flaws openly and without judgement, neither over-identifying ("I'm a loser") nor suppressing ("suck it up")Getting lost in the negative story

Practical tools from the lesson:

  • Treat yourself as you would treat a dear friend.
  • Comforting touch: safe touch soothes the nervous system (a massage, a hug, or a weighted blanket if no one is around).
  • Mindfulness practices: meditation, mindful eating or walking, journaling.
  • Treat emotions as information: "What are my feelings trying to tell me?" Meeting reactions with curiosity over criticism breaks the spiral of self-judgement and builds emotional intelligence.
  • Practising on yourself makes you better at meeting clients' struggles with compassion.

Shame (Lesson 54)

  • The lesson quotes a researcher's definition (the name is cut off in the transcript): shame is an intensely painful feeling of believing we are flawed and therefore unworthy of love and belonging.
  • Three primary factors around shame: feeling trapped, powerless and isolated.
  • It can show up in any wellness area: nutrition, movement, emotional wellbeing.

How shame shows up in sessions

  • Distress at not being "allowed" to have faults, mess up, or even want change
  • Feeling unworthy of help or attention, or worrying they are boring the coach by repeating themselves
  • Limiting beliefs
  • Self-deprecating comments wrapped in humour
  • Anger, overwhelm, frustration, turning inward or shutting down

Shame lowers belief in the capacity to change and erodes trust in oneself, the coach and the process.

Empathy as the remedy

The expert calls empathy the "kryptonite" to shame: putting yourself in someone's shoes and caring about their experience.

  • Acknowledge it. When a client names shame, they have given you a gift of vulnerability. A simple affirmation that they've been heard can be healing.
  • Follow their lead and give them agency, while holding the container.
  • Don't rush. Their goals can be recognised without pushing towards them.
  • Coaching practices that help: engaged listening; respecting vulnerability; reinforcing worthiness, belonging and capacity; helping them notice and name when and why shame shows up; exploring social and cultural influences; asking what is on the other side of shame; asking whether shame is serving them; anchoring back to their strengths and values.
  • Shame resilience theory includes a relational element: being seen in your vulnerability and receiving empathy. Over time clients start coaching themselves.
  • Holding the client capable and able (generative accountability) also works well with shame. See Coaching Mindset.

Self-compassion is not self-indulgence (Lesson 55)

  • Make the self-criticism or shame the object of compassion. Most people find compassion for others easier than for themselves.
  • Shame or self-criticism is a sign that you care. Instead of caring less to escape the discomfort, double down on what matters.
  • Write down the words you would say to someone you care about, then say them to yourself.
  • Erase the slate: look for the very next action that fits the goal or value, and start again.
  • Compassion means seeing pain, wanting to help, and having the courage and discernment to respond in a way that actually helps. It is not reaching for a bad habit to make the feeling go away.

Coaching in practice

  • "If a close friend told you this, what would you say to them?"
  • "Thank you for sharing that with me. I know that isn't easy to say."
  • "It sounds like this matters a lot to you. That feeling might be a sign of how much you care."
  • "What's the very next small thing you could do that reflects this goal?"
  • "Is that voice helping you right now? Where do you think it comes from?"
  • "What strengths have carried you through hard times before?"

Quotes

"Treat yourself as you'd treat a dear friend." (Lesson 9)

"I think of empathy as the kryptonite to shame." (Lesson 54)

"Self-compassion is not self-indulgence." (Lesson 55)

Self-Efficacy

Lesson covered: 52 (Self-Efficacy Factors). Self-efficacy also runs through lessons 7, 10, 60, 69 and 71.

Key ideas

  • The end goal of coaching is to build self-efficacy: the ability to become confident, self-aware and intentional about keeping up healthy habits on your own. Coaching is not about holding hands forever.
  • Each client arrives with a unique mix of efficacies: the things they see themselves as good at. Someone may have high efficacy at work or in family life but low efficacy with nutrition and lifestyle.
  • The lesson builds self-efficacy from four factors: feedback, experiences, limiting factors and capacity.

The four factors

1. Feedback

Feedback is not instructing. Instructing gives evidence-based information. Feedback holds up a mirror so clients see themselves more clearly: their behaviour, outcomes and progress.

TypeDescription
ObjectiveBased on data; presents measurable information
SubjectiveYour opinion and what it's based on, followed by asking what the client thinks or feels about it
SummativeA periodic review of feedback after a period of working together
PositiveRecognising what is going well
CriticalAddresses what's needed to progress. Never "negative"; always make clear it's about behaviour, not their value as a person

Feedback builds trust. As self-efficacy grows, even objective feedback starts to feel like information rather than a personal failing.

2. Experiences

Experiences are even more powerful than feedback.

TypeDescription
Mastery experiencesThe client builds on previous success and sees tangible evidence that success is possible. This grows their sense of skill
Past experiencesShape how the client reads their current life and can open the way to new experiences
Vicarious experiencesIdentifying with someone else's story, either an inspiring success or a cautionary tale

The cycle: reflection, guidance and goal setting → new choices → process them with the coach → new mastery experiences → more self-efficacy.

3. Limiting factors

Everyone has some. Strategise to work through or around them. The course sorts them into three types:

  • Fixed: cannot be changed
  • Malleable: can be adjusted
  • Perceived: may not really be a limitation at all

4. Capacity

The client's ability to identify goals and motivations, map a route to them, and engage in positive behaviours along the way. Capacity depends on skill level, past experience and motivation for change.

Self-efficacy elsewhere in the course

  • Locus of control (Lesson 7): identifying controllable behaviours leads towards self-efficacy.
  • Motivational interviewing (Lesson 10): supporting autonomy and competence is a catalyst for self-efficacy.
  • Coaching continuum (Lesson 60): coaching differs for low- vs high-efficacy clients.
  • Wellness plan (Lesson 69): realistic goals clients are confident they can hit keep improving self-efficacy.
  • Monitoring (Lesson 71): recognising achievements gives self-efficacy an automatic boost.

Coaching in practice

  • "Can I share what I've noticed over the last few weeks?" (summative feedback)
  • "Here's what the numbers show. What do you make of that?" (objective feedback, then their interpretation)
  • "This is about the routine, not about you as a person. Let's look at what got in the way."
  • "Tell me about a time you succeeded at something like this before."
  • "Is that something that truly can't change, or something we could work around?"
  • "Is there someone whose story inspires you here?"

Quotes

"Coaching isn't about holding your client's hands day after day until the end of time." (Lesson 52)

"It's more like holding up a mirror for your clients to see themselves more clearly." (Lesson 52)

Wellness Mindset: "Get To" vs "Have To"

Lessons covered: 70 (Wellness Mindset). Lesson 53 is titled "Get to vs Have to", but its transcript is about meaning in movement; see Movement Motivation.

Key ideas

  • Wellness is about getting to do things that feel good and make you feel good about yourself, your community and life. It is not about forcing yourself to do things that are good for you.
  • It is a process of self-discovery: following what feels good and right, and finding what helps you thrive.
  • What sets this apart from many health and fitness approaches: it is not about being told what you have to do, and not about shortcuts.
  • It stands for freedom and choice, hope and possibility, learning to trust and care for yourself.
  • The coach's goal is to help clients discover what creates health, happiness and meaning for them, not to get them to do what someone else has decided is good for them.

Why it matters:

  • Clients feel less resistance to trying new things and making changes.
  • After a setback there is less shame, so they are less likely to withdraw from their goals or from coaching.
  • Their whole wellness journey feels more positive and meaningful.

From vision to choice points

  1. Build a personal wellness vision. Lift the conversation above a number on a scale or a vague wish to "feel better". (Questions below.)
  2. Apply the vision to each domain. Ask clients to describe their ideal relationship with a domain such as food, movement or sleep.
  3. Find choice points. Pick an existing routine or time of day and ask what would make it better or more consistent with their vision.
  4. Co-create a small commitment they can try out to see whether it supports them. Share research-supported ideas, but encourage their creativity. Don't hand over a generic fix-it list.

The empowering coach–client relationship

  • Be a guide more than an expert: help clients find their own direction and then navigate it.
  • It starts with respect, empathy and curiosity. Listen and learn from what they share.
  • Balance evidence-based suggestions with encouraging clients to trust their own direct experience. You won't always know what is best, and the client is in charge of their wellness.
  • Root it in positive social connection: see the good in them, commiserate and celebrate, and hold hope when they are frustrated or unsure.
  • Each contact should leave them feeling understood, encouraged and more confident.

Your own mindset is contagious

The lesson opens by asking what you do for your own wellness that you enjoy and are grateful for. Mindsets are contagious, so a coach needs to exude hope, gratitude and self-trust. Keep reflecting on your own wellness journey. Role-modelling the mindset is a source of both empathy and inspiration.

  • "What would wellness feel like? What would be different in your life?"

  • "What roles, relationships and goals matter most to you?"
  • "Describe your ideal relationship with food (or movement, or sleep)."
  • "Think about your mornings. What could make them a little more like that vision?"
  • Reframe: "What if this isn't something you have to do, but something you get to do?"

Psychological and Behavioral Flexibility

Key ideas

  • Living in the moment means focusing on the now, in body and mind, without the mind wandering to to-do lists or the next meal.
  • Psychological and behavioral flexibility is the ability to recognise and adapt to what a situation demands while staying open and aware. The lesson calls it the "superpower" that lets you live in the now.
  • It brings greater self-awareness and the ability to pause and notice what is happening.
  • Your presence is contagious: a coach's ability to stay present affects the client's ability to stay present.
  • Bringing the client back to the meaning behind a goal helps them remember their why.

Upward Spirals and the Transfer Effect

Lessons covered: 58 (Upward Spirals), 59 (Upward Spirals Q&A), 18 (Incorporating Movement: purpose and activity)

Key ideas

  • An upward spiral is a psychological phenomenon in which one positive behaviour makes another more likely, creating a positive feedback loop.
  • Example from the lesson: a good night's sleep → you feel better → you're more likely to eat well → you have energy to be active → you probably sleep better. All of it lifts mood; sustained long enough, it lifts disposition.
  • The theory is based on research by Barbara Fredrickson and her team.

Why positive emotions matter: broadening

Negative emotions trigger specific, narrow impulses. Positive emotions broaden thinking, making people more mentally flexible, creative, cooperative and open to new experiences.

EmotionImpulse it triggers
AngerLash out
FearFlee
JoyPlayfulness, exploration
LoveCuriosity, vulnerability, connection
GratitudeEndearment, reciprocity
HopeProblem-solving, tenacity
Interest, amusementCreativity

Even when someone doesn't act on a negative impulse, brain imaging shows the impulse is still there. Positive emotion brings neurological flexibility, which makes lasting change possible. The lesson's point: specific behaviours matter, but it is the positive emotions that broaden and change the brain.

Documented upward spirals (as listed in Lesson 58)

  • Self-concordant goals ↔ reaching goals ↔ happiness
  • Positive emotions ↔ vagal tone
  • Loving-kindness meditation ↔ mood
  • Gratitude practice ↔ humility
  • Positive connections with others ↔ positive disposition and meaning
  • Going for walks ↔ good mood
  • Healthy behaviours (exercise, eating well) ↔ good mood
  • Sense of purpose ↔ physical activity

Purpose and physical activity (Lesson 18)

A study of about 14,000 Americans measured physical activity and sense of purpose, then checked again four years later. Regularly active people showed a greater increase in purpose, and people with a sense of purpose at the start were more likely to exercise regularly. The relationship runs both ways: taking care of yourself helps you find a reason to, and having a big why helps you take care of yourself.

The transfer effect

When someone makes a positive change in one area, it spreads into others. The skills of change and the gains in resilience give a boost elsewhere. Examples from the course:

  • Better sleep → better mood → kinder to people → better relationships
  • Focusing on sleep → eating better
  • Eating right → working out harder
  • Working on mindfulness and mindset → doubling down on sleep

Incremental change (Lesson 59)

Most people need small changes linked together. One small success leads to a second, third and fourth, and over time these add up to big change. Examples the expert gives:

  • Drink more water each day
  • Deep breathing for 3 minutes, twice a day, to reduce stress
  • Pick two vegetables a day

The coach reinforces each small change so it becomes a sustained behaviour, and the lifestyle the client came for follows.

Coaching in practice

  • "Since you started sleeping better, have you noticed anything else changing?"
  • "What's one small win from this week we can build on?"
  • "Which change feels like it would make the others easier?"
  • "How did you feel after that walk?" (savouring the positive emotion strengthens the loop)
  • "What gives you a sense of purpose? How might moving more connect to that?"

Benefits of Movement

Lessons covered: 12 (Physical Activity & Wellness), 13 (Muscle), 14 (Sedentary Behaviors), 15 (Movement: Mental & Emotional)

Everyday movement counts (Lesson 12)

  • Regular movement through the day is a cornerstone of any wellness strategy: "use it or lose it".
  • Activities of daily living count: household chores, standing during TV adverts, pacing while on the phone.
  • Benefits can start before any structured exercise: better sleep, immune function, and heart and metabolic function.
  • Simply shifting a client's relationship with movement and their awareness of its benefits can contribute to positive physiological change.
  • The big practical finding: interrupting prolonged sitting with brief light-to-moderate movement about every 30 minutes has been shown to improve a range of physiological functions. No gym, equipment or heavy weights needed.

Risks of too little movement (as listed in the course)

AdultsType 2 diabetes; cardiovascular disease incidence and mortality; moderate relative risk for ovarian, colon and endometrial cancers; more depression, anxiety and stress; lower cognitive function
Young peopleStrong evidence for obesity; moderate evidence for hypertension and raised total cholesterol; low self-esteem, social and behavioural problems, lower fitness and academic achievement

Screen time outside work and school is noted as an added concern.

Muscle (Lesson 13)

  • Muscle is called "the currency of aging" and described as the body's biggest endocrine organ: it makes hormones and responds to hormonal signals.
  • The goal is not just building muscle but maintaining lean muscle mass with age, for resilience, work capacity and quality of life.

Sedentary behaviour (Lesson 14)

  • "Sitting is the new smoking"? The expert says sitting itself isn't, but sedentary behaviours probably are: epidemiological data show a pattern similar to smoking.
  • Sedentary = seated, reclining or lying down. People who sit more have a higher relative risk of heart disease, cancer and metabolic disease (metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes).
  • Exercisers can still be sedentary. If someone spends most of the day in those positions, exercise does not undo it.
  • Takeaway: not "exercise more", but get up, walk around, sit and recline less. Break up sitting with standing or walking.
  • Work out with clients what they believe they can do. When it's collaborative, they're far more likely to stick with it.

Moving for wellness focuses on feelings, enjoyment and mood. Moving for fitness uses similar methods with a different aim.

Positive psychology (Lesson 19)

  • Positive psychology is the scientific study of human wellbeing.
  • Traditional psychology studied dysfunction: mental illness and disorders such as anxiety, depression and schizophrenia. The founders of positive psychology noticed that the absence of disease is not the same as the presence of wellbeing, and asked what makes a fulfilling life.
  • Researchers studied people with high life satisfaction, drawing on everything from ancient philosophy to modern self-help, to find what mattered to people who were doing well.

  • Healthy behaviours that connect us to others are more likely to be repeated.

Stress Management and Recovery

Lessons covered: 21 (Stress Management), 22 (Stress Q&A), 23 (Stress & Recovery), 32 (Stress Reduction Strategies)

What stress is

  • Stress is what happens when the body or mind meets a demanding situation or stimulus. Emotional stress can change brain and hormonal activity (Lesson 32).
  • Sources can be external (finances, work, relationship changes) or internal (illness, fear, poor nutrition).
  • The fight-or-flight response is an evolved, primal reaction, well adapted to acute threats to life and safety (Lesson 21).
  • Our brains are wired to notice negatives (see Negativity Bias). That kept ancestors alive, but biology hasn't kept up with technology.
  • The real problem for modern people is chronic, perceived emotional stress, especially when it is unpredictable and uncontrollable. We are also very good at ruminating on it.
  • Because most modern stress is psychological and perceived, we have more control over it than we might think.

Allostatic load (Lesson 32)

Stressors that chronically activate the stress response create allostatic load: the cumulative wear and tear of stress on the body. A heavy load can erode muscles, bones, organs and brain tissue. Chronic stress, even at low daily levels, is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease, autoimmune disorders, anxiety and depression. Returning to homeostasis and managing allostatic load are essential to living well.

Not all stress is bad

  • Eustress (positive stress) drives growth and sometimes needs to increase to improve health and fitness. A hard workout stresses the body but may destress the mind (Lesson 23).
  • Some pressure improves performance. Skill, personality, confidence and task complexity all affect how each person handles it (Lesson 32).

The stress management toolbox (Lesson 21)

Other coping activities (Lesson 32): grounding, reading, music (helps return the body to homeostasis), journaling, gratitude practice and hobbies.

Emotional pain is real pain (Lesson 22)

  • Language across cultures describes emotional hurt as pain. The brain largely uses the same circuitry for physical and emotional pain.
  • In lab conditions, acetaminophen also reduced emotional pain responses, which the expert offers as evidence that emotional pain is real.
  • Be gentle with each other and with yourself.
  • Gratitude and compassion meditation have short-term mood effects and, with practice, may change the brain towards being "happier at rest" through neuroplasticity.
  • Vagal tone (related to the vagus nerve, the 10th cranial nerve, which reaches most major organs) is described as a measure of stress, and there is an upward spiral between positive emotion and vagal tone.

Recovery and regeneration (Lesson 23)

  • Mindfulness, quality sleep, time with friends and family, and healthy dietary patterns reduce stress, improve wellbeing and restore readiness for new stressors.
  • Recovery cannot happen without stress; without stress there is nothing to recover from.
  • Key recovery pillars: sleep, nutrition and quiet wakefulness.
  • Life is messy. The aim is not to remove all stress but to balance stress and stress relief for good health, wellness and performance.

  • "How much of this is happening now, and how much is replaying or worrying ahead?"
  • "Which of these, breathing, music, a walk, journaling, would you be willing to try this week?"
  • "What does recovery look like in your week? Where is your quiet time?"

Performance, Cognition and the Mind–Body Connection

Lessons covered: 24 (Performance & Cognition), 25 (Performance & Cognition Q&A), 26 (Mind–Body Connection)

Arousal and performance: the inverted U (Lesson 24)

Arousal levelWhat happensPerformance
Too lowSleepy, boredSuffers

Why too much hurts: stress hormones bind to brain areas needed for performance, especially the hippocampus (which has cortisol receptors). The hippocampus is the learning and memory hub, and it also helps shut down the stress response. When it is saturated, you can't recall things in the moment and the stress response is harder to switch off.

Getting back to the sweet spot: reframe or reappraise the situation, or use stress-reduction tools such as deep breathing.

Cognitive load and attention

  • Cognitive resources are limited. Divide attention and every task suffers.
  • Attention is our most precious resource: the most costly thing for the brain to do, and something it doesn't like doing. People spend about half their lives mind-wandering.
  • Learning to point the attentional spotlight at the task at hand is a big advantage.
  • Mind and body are linked: when cognitive load rises, musculoskeletal load rises too. Muscles tense without our awareness. On busy days, suggest short breaks for progressive relaxation or deep breathing.

Mindset, cognition and emotion

  • Fixed vs growth mindset affects learning and performance; mindsets can change (see Growth Mindset).
  • Emotion always influences cognition, and cognition can help regulate emotion (down-regulating unhelpful emotions).
StrategyWhat it isEffectiveness (per the course)
Cognitive reappraisalMentally changing how you interpret an emotional event to lessen its impactUsually effective
SuppressionPushing the emotion downUsually not effective

When the frontal lobe is flooded with stress hormones it doesn't work well, which makes self-control harder.

Mindfulness and the reward system (Lesson 25)

  • Mindfulness (awareness of where your attention is) is among the best things for cognitive health. Remembering a happy moment shows you were present then.
  • The expert admits being a chronic mind-wanderer: mindfulness is a practice, not perfection.
  • The brain is hardwired for survival drives. The hypothalamus drives the "four Fs" (fight, flight, food and sex) with or without our permission.
  • Survival-related behaviours are rewarded with dopamine via the mesolimbic dopaminergic pathway: ventral tegmental area → nucleus accumbens → prefrontal cortex. Carbohydrate-rich treats may also release serotonin.
  • Our ancestors survived by preferring high-fat, high-calorie food. With unlimited access today, we sometimes need to rein that in.

Embodied cognition (Lesson 26)

ViewIdea
Traditional cognitive viewThe body sends sensory input to the brain; the brain sends motor output
Embodied cognitionThe whole body influences cognition: it thinks, processes and produces behaviour in its own way
  • Example: walking comes largely from how the body is built. The brain may handle navigation, but the body adjusts pace and balance automatically.
  • Emotions are felt in the whole body ("a punch in the gut", "a slap in the face").
  • For coaching: consider how a client's physical and mental worlds work together to carry out a wellness behaviour, and help them feel physically and mentally connected.

Sleep Hygiene and Optimizing Sleep

Key ideas

  • Sleep is the "Swiss Army knife of health": whatever is going on, sleep makes it a little better.
  • Sleep is basic hygiene, like brushing your teeth or showering. Nobody boasts about skipping those because they're busy, so we shouldn't say it about sleep.

Healthy Dietary Patterns: Food First

Lessons covered: 34 (Healthy Diet), 35 (Cooking), 36 (Food vs Nutrients), 37 (Dietary Patterns), 38 (Q&A: Autophagy), 39 (Supporting Healthy Eating), 40 (Client Questions)

Rethinking "healthy diet" (Lesson 34)

  • "Healthy" and "diet" together often cause conflicting feelings. Wellness is holistic and shouldn't include stress or shame about food.
  • A diet built on exact calories, macros and micros is extremely restrictive. It can cause stress, preoccupation with food, cutting out favourite foods, and skipping social occasions.
  • A truly healthy diet considers factors beyond nutritional value: lifestyle, social circle, culture, emotional state.
  • Dietary pattern (the definition quoted in the course): the totality of what someone habitually eats and drinks, with the parts acting synergistically on health.
  • Goal: an overall pattern that, on average, has a net positive effect on health, rather than fixating on single nutrients, foods or meals.
  • Positive psychological outcomes come from enjoying taste, being included in social food gatherings, and eating in line with cultural and moral values without guilt or shame.

Food vs nutrients (Lesson 36)

  • Nutrition is the process of taking in food and using its energy and nutrients for growth and repair: ingestion, digestion, absorption, transportation, assimilation and excretion.
  • There is no single optimal, universal human diet. Humans adapted to every environment; diets are shaped by region, climate, culture, religion, food availability, socioeconomics and preference.
  • Healthy patterns share traits:
Lower inHigher in
Processed foodsFibre
Animal fatFruits and vegetables
Refined grainsSeafood, dairy
Added sugarsNuts and legumes
  • Why food first: patterns allow many routes to a healthy diet and translate easily into eating behaviours, which are practical and easier to implement than nutrient targets.

Saturated fat example: high saturated fat raises heart-disease risk, but the strength of that link depends on the rest of the diet. Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat gives the biggest risk reduction. Replacing it with refined carbs and added sugar gives no change. What replaces a nutrient matters.

Flexibility: the 80–90% idea (Lesson 37)

  • A healthy pattern means flexibility, not rigid rules ("I can only eat salmon, I can only eat salad"), which tend to break people.
  • Expand variety: more vegetables, different legumes, suitable grains, varied proteins.
  • Focus on what you do 80–90% of the time, and allow room for social eating and holidays.
  • Programs must work for where people are: their culture, city and neighbourhood.
  • When people drift, use empathy and nudge them back to the pattern.

Night-time repair and autophagy (Lesson 38, Q&A)

  • For most people, night is repair time: processing the day's nutrients, rebuilding and rejuvenating. Hormones follow a circadian rhythm.
  • Autophagy is described as the body's way of cleaning up waste products of metabolism, "carrying out the trash" at the cellular level.
  • Acute inflammation after a workout is normal and switches off. Overtraining, overeating, stress and poor sleep push towards chronic inflammation.
  • Constant snacking and late eating may disrupt the shift into repair and clean-up.
  • The expert suggests a roughly 12-hour eating window (breakfast, lunch, dinner), perhaps 14 hours fasting and 10 eating, occasionally 16 hours, for digestive rest.
  • The expert also flags fibre as something modern diets lose.

Cooking as a wellness behaviour (Lesson 35)

  • In the US, home food preparation fell 23% between 1965 and 2007, while eating away from home rose 42%.
  • Eating more home-prepared food is linked to lower risk of lifestyle diseases (type 2 diabetes, heart disease).
  • Learning to cook is associated with lower anxiety and depression, higher self-rated happiness and more resilience.
  • Cooking can bring connection, positive emotion, accomplishment, purpose and self-nourishment. Culinary medicine (Harvard is cited) teaches patients cooking and nutrition.
  • Invite clients to consider how food gets from the ground to their body, and what values (local sourcing, climate, culture, tradition) they could express through food.

The coach's role in eating (Lessons 39–40)

  • Eating well is challenging and is the basis for wellness. Be supportive, empathetic and guiding.
  • Be the "strong tethering pole" or anchor when clients fall off the wagon.
  • Aim for consistency and a long health span, not a quick fix or temporary diet.
  • Use science to help clients cut through the noise and focus on what matters.

Coaching in practice

  • "Tell me about what a typical day of eating looks like for you."
  • "What foods do you love? Which ones connect you to your culture or family?"
  • "What would it look like if 80–90% of your meals supported how you want to feel?"
  • "What's one new vegetable or legume you'd be curious to try?"
  • "How do you feel about cooking? What's one simple meal you'd like to learn?"
  • "When do you usually stop eating in the evening?"

Eating, Emotions and Body Weight

Lessons covered: 41 (Eating & Emotional Wellbeing), 42 (Weight-Neutral Approach), 43 (Intuitive Eating)

Eating and emotional wellbeing (Lesson 41)

The social side of food

  • Long-term health effects of diet include food's psychological importance.
  • Mediterranean-diet research is cited: the social support, community, relaxation and enjoyment of sharing food stimulate enjoyment of healthy diets. Even when the food isn't "healthy", the social experience brings health benefits.
  • Eating together may have evolved to support social bonding.
  • Within a healthy overall pattern, a meal or a day of discretionary eating has more positive than negative effects.

Stress and eating

  • Stress can cause under-eating in some people and over-eating in others.
  • Acute and chronic stress can drive cravings for energy-dense, hyper-palatable foods for self-soothing.
  • Work, personal life and stress-management skills can be barriers to change.

Mindful eating

Paying attention to hunger, fullness, taste satisfaction, cravings, emotional eating and other triggers, with self-acceptance. Research cited suggests it may reduce visceral fat, cortisol and later over-snacking.

Dichotomous thinking and disinhibited eating

  • Dichotomous thinking means seeing foods as purely "good" or "bad".
  • It can lead to disinhibited eating: after "breaking a rule", the person overeats in response.
  • Teach that no single food is good or bad. The overall pattern matters most. Watch for inflexible food rules.

Reward-driven eating

  • The brain's reward system drives wanting food (dopamine) and enjoying it in the moment (opioids and cannabinoids).
  • "Food addiction" is a term applied to people more susceptible to a reward response to hyper-palatable foods. Signs to watch for: severe binges and cravings, high responsiveness to palatable foods, impulsivity, addictive personality traits.

Definition: eating based on physiological hunger and satiety cues rather than situational and emotional cues. It is directly linked with psychological wellbeing.

Four core features

  1. Unconditional permission to eat: eat when hungry, no guilt or shame, no "good" or "bad" foods.
  2. Eat for physical, not emotional, reasons: eat when hungry, stop when full; interrupt cycles of using food to cope with distress.

It seems most effective for binge eating, emotional eating and eating in response to external cues. Tools to offer clients: intuitive eating, mindful eating and the hunger scale.

Motivational Interviewing and the Coaching Continuum

What motivational interviewing is

  • Motivational interviewing (MI) is a client-centred communication style for understanding a client's priorities and building an action plan. It is especially useful with clients who are ambivalent about change (Lesson 10).
  • Its fundamentals: ask the right questions, listen actively and understand the reply, then respond with constructive information or advice (Lesson 10).
  • It targets resistance to change and lets change feel organic and collaborative.
  • MI is linked to self-determination theory, the idea that humans inherently seek growth. Supporting autonomy and a feeling of competence can be a powerful catalyst for self-efficacy (Lesson 10).
  • It starts with the client feeling accepted and heard without judgement, and without being handed advice straight away. Trust comes before feedback (Lesson 56).
  • Being heard opens the client up to talk about what they really want to change and why, which leads to commitment to the eventual plan (Lesson 56).

What the coaching mindset is

The coaching mindset is a framework for how you engage in and approach your work. It is:

  • Client-centred, rooted in inquiry and empathy, in partnership and collaboration
  • Grounded in your self-awareness and commitment to the process
  • Based on trust: in the client's wisdom, experience, pace and capacity for change; the client's trust in you and in the space you create; and your trust in yourself and the process

You are not there to fix, direct or know everything. You are there to ask, listen, support, reflect and affirm (Lesson 67).

Holding clients capable and able: generative accountability

Traditional "accountability" can feel like nagging or obligation ("you did something wrong, you owe me"). The course reframes it:

  • The goals come from the client's own desires and vision, so buy-in and choice are built in.
  • The coach believes the client can do it, sometimes more than the client does. That models efficacy.
  • Generative accountability grows intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy: it is growthful, engaged and mutual.
  • When something doesn't happen, get curious: what worked, what didn't, what needs to shift? A "missed opportunity" becomes an opportunity for discovery.

Imposter feelings (Lessons 50 and 66)

  • New coaches often feel the imposter experience: doubting whether they are "enough".
  • You don't have to be perfect. You can serve from exactly where you are.
  • Imposter syndrome involves some shame and fear, and vulnerability helps: share your concern with people you trust and be open to feedback.
  • Let good feedback sink in. People in service roles often struggle to accept it.
  • The best cure is to get out there and use your skills to build someone else up.

The coach as mirror (Lesson 66)

  • The client does the work. The coach holds up a mirror and reflects their words back in new ways so they can see themselves from other angles.
  • We all have blind spots. Reflection is "the real meat of coaching", the part nobody can find by searching the internet.
  • Don't tell new clients what their transformation will look like. Telling can cloud what they think is possible and make the journey a straight-line "dart throw". Open a space to explore: clients often discover things they didn't know they wanted, or didn't want.
  • On movement: even when prescribing exercise, the goal is an independent practice outside sessions. Balance training time with coaching conversations, and keep goals and expectations clear.

The relationship is the vehicle (Lesson 51)

Remember how it feels when someone supports you wholeheartedly, listens and gives you space to reflect. Clients could get instructions anywhere. What is rare is someone who cares, believes in them and holds a vision for them through self-doubt. Build the relationship first; it is the vessel that carries your evidence-based advice.

Aim of every session: the client feels heard, respected and supported, with forward momentum on their wellness goals. You don't need all the answers.

Consistent session structure (Lesson 68)

  • Consistency builds trust and rapport. Offer a consistent coaching container so the client feels ease, confidence and dependability.
  • Standardise the components: how you open and close sessions and how you check in on goals and strategies.
  • The structure signals that this is intentional time, not a chat with a friend. It helps the client move from the chaos of their day into honesty and receptivity.
  • Consistent doesn't mean rigid. Stay personalised and responsive; adapt as you gain experience; ask for feedback on what is working.
  • Look after yourself as an essential resource: leave time for breaks.
  • Think long-term: goal timelines built on small, achievable wins that lead to bigger ones.
  • Respect the client's time.

Coaching in practice

  • "I know you really want to do this. How can I support you?"
  • "That didn't go as planned. What did you learn about what works for you?"
  • "What do you want to be true by the end of our time today?" (consistent opener)
  • "What are you taking away, and what's your next step before we meet again?" (consistent close)
  • "How is the way we work together going for you? Anything you'd change?"
  • Before a session, ask yourself: "What am I bringing in with me today, and what do I need to set down?"

Cultural Awareness and Coaching Philosophy

Lessons covered: 62 (Cultural Awareness), 63 (Coaching Philosophy), 64 (Creating a Coaching Philosophy 1), 65 (Creating a Coaching Philosophy 2: no transcript available)

Both topics start from the same place: knowing your own baseline (your background, identity, culture, values and relationship to wellness) so you can show up well for clients.

Cultural awareness (Lesson 62)

  • Everyone brings unique life experiences, identities and values into sessions. Coaching happens inside a larger cultural context that shapes each person's relationship to wellness.
  • Recognising this in clients and in yourself helps you coach with curiosity, release your ego and centre the client. The aim is not to deny your own perceptions but to honour differences.
  • Culturally responsive coaching: clients feel safe bringing the whole of their identities and experiences and know they'll be received with care. You don't have to agree with or even understand them, but you must coach with curiosity and respect.

Establishing your baseline

Your professional background, life experiences, identity, culture and relationship to wellness all shape how you show up. Knowing what is true and normal for you helps you notice your own assumptions and projections, and move from default mode into reflective curiosity.

Identity markers place a person closer to or further from power and privilege. This is constructed at the societal level, and both coach and client likely hold a mix of dominant and non-dominant identities.

Bias

TypeDefinition
Implicit (unconscious) biasStereotypes or judgements you hold without awareness that shape your responses
Explicit (conscious) biasJudgements you hold with deliberate awareness

Everyone has both. Unchecked, they keep you in unhelpful patterns or cause unintentional harm. When it happens: apologise and repair, and offer yourself compassion without self-judgement. Make room for genuine inquiry without projection.

Emotional triggers

Past experiences and implicit bias can trigger a fight-or-flight reaction in session. To come back to the present: deep breathing, feeling your feet on the ground, a hand on your belly or heart, or quiet self-talk. Keep the focus on the client so you respond rather than react.

Equity and communication culture

  • Equity awareness: people don't start from the same place. Environmental and social factors shape everyone's relationship to wellness.
  • Coaching communication culture: the intentional way you relate to each client. It combines your natural, authentic style, your training, and the client's communication needs and preferences, using active and reflective listening, skilful questions and thoughtful responses.

Coaching philosophy (Lessons 63–64)

  • Every coach has a philosophy; it's in how they work and why they're in the field. Most never put it into words.
  • An official coaching philosophy sets out the core values and approaches behind your unique way of coaching.
  • It can be the first step in building trust and rapport, connecting potential clients to the right coach. "Nobody's everybody": not everyone will resonate with you, and that's fine.
  • It may be the bedrock of your brand and business, because it is you. What makes you unique is what draws people in. New coaches tend to hold back what's unique to them, but clients are looking for personal connection, not just a list of skills.
  • It is not static. It grows and changes as you do; revisit it.
  • Writing it down builds confidence, draws in the people you want to serve and makes clear what sets you apart.
  • You don't have to be "finished" or an expert in everything you coach. Your own wellness journey makes you a more empathic coach.

Creating, Monitoring and Changing the Wellness Plan

Lessons covered: 69 (Creating a Wellness Plan), 71 (Monitoring and Its Importance), 72 (Changing the Wellness Plan); vision questions from 70 (Wellness Mindset)

Creating the plan (Lesson 69)

  • The client comes to you, but the client directs the process. The plan is client-directed and collaborative, built around their desires and value-centred goals.
  • If a client says "just tell me what to do", remind them that your role is to help them tap into their own strength and self-awareness. They know themselves best. Redirect them to their wellness vision, their intake assessments and forms, and use motivational interviewing.
  • Realistic goals they are confident they can reach keep building self-efficacy. Every win counts, and it counts more when the idea comes from the client.
  • The most important input is the scope of the wellness vision. The plan must line up with the vision, so every step visibly moves them closer. The vision will change over time, so stay open.
  • Identify the support system: family and friends help commitment outside sessions.

Plan document structure

SectionContents
TopThe client's wellness vision, in their words
Short-term goalsNear-term goals that serve the vision
Action stepsSmall steps the client can confidently carry out between sessions
SupportsWho and what helps (or hinders)

It can be on paper or digital.

Session rhythm

  • Sessions are typically once a week. That leaves time to put commitments into practice, see how support systems help or hinder, and discover strengths.
  • At the next session: share how it went, celebrate wins, and decide what to explore further.

Vision questions (Lesson 70)

"What brought you here?" · "What is your definition of wellness?" · "What would be different in your life?" · "What's holding you back?" · "What would wellness be in service of?" See Get To vs Have To for the full list.

Monitoring (Lesson 71)

Monitoring is the systematic, continuous collection of observations and data: tracking the client's experiences while holding them capable and able.

What regular check-ins do:

  • Give time to reflect on what's working and celebrate victories. Recognising achievements gives self-efficacy an automatic boost.
  • Identify what made progress happen.
  • Discuss what's been challenging, and adjust the process, goals or action steps.
  • Review the client's self-awareness of their tendencies, motivations and understanding, not just your observations.
  • Spot shifts in what's truly meaningful to the client.

Tools: motivational interviewing and open-ended questions; assessment tools on self-reflection and mindfulness; questionnaires on specific wellness areas.

Why it matters: people often "can't see the forest for the trees". An outside view and objective encouragement help them see how far they've come.

Changing the plan (Lesson 72)

  • The only constant is change. In coaching, change is normal, expected and a sign of growth.
  • Example from the lesson: a client starts out wanting to lose weight, but over time it emerges that the real goal is better balance between work, sleep and overall health.

How to change the plan well:

  1. Discuss together how the current plan will be affected before starting a new path.
  2. Build on what's already in motion. What has the client learned that carries over? Which strengths will help with new goals?
  3. Look at earlier assessments for ideas.
  4. Keep every change client-centred and co-created.
  5. Rebalance goals so focus doesn't lean too heavily on one goal or start too long-term. Lead with small victories, especially when short-term goals need intense focus.
  6. Normalise and celebrate change. Treat it as a win and a sign of greater possibility, not a failure.

Coaching in practice

  • "Let's write your vision at the top, in your own words."
  • "What's one step you're confident you can take before we meet next week?" Then: "How confident, 1 to 10?"
  • "Who in your life could support this?"
  • "What went well this week? What made that possible?"
  • "What was hardest? What might we adjust?"
  • "Looking back to when we started, what's different now?"
  • "It sounds like what matters to you has shifted. Shall we revisit the plan together?"

Situations That Derail

Lesson covered: 73 (Situations that Derail)

Key ideas

  • Not every session goes smoothly. Change is hard, and people react differently to being pushed out of their comfort zone.
  • Most clients are glad to take part, but some behaviours challenge the coaching relationship.
  • These are normal. Put them in context and appreciate what the client may be going through.
  • A person's actions almost always reflect an underlying need or struggle. When people can't voice a need or inner conflict, it often comes out as discordant behaviour. When something isn't working, people fall back on familiar, comfortable behaviour.

Common derailers

BehaviourWhat may be underneath
Trouble with follow-throughOverwhelm; a plan that isn't realistic yet
Hesitation to try new thingsFear, discomfort with change
Stuck: repeating unproductive behaviours despite agreeing to changeFalling back on the familiar when something isn't working
Low motivation or self-efficacyNot yet connected to their real why
Behaviour that feels unreceptive or disrespectfulFeeling challenged or anxious because of internal or external factors; changes in environment or support

How to respond

  1. Stay curious, empathetic and kind. Use motivational interviewing and reflective listening.
  2. Stay within your scope of practice. Open communication makes it easier to set and keep boundaries.
  3. Name what you notice, neutrally. Use statements that leave room for the client's own perspective. Feeling heard helps turn things around.
  4. Use reflective listening, including amplified reflection (below).
  5. Take it in without taking it on. Acknowledge their feelings and clarify what's behind them without getting defensive. It is probably not about you; it may be how they're processing change.
  6. Validate emotions about what's changing in their world, then explore ways to address their concerns and re-engage together.
  7. Use feedback to adjust. Their reaction may show you how to meet them where they are and sharpen your skills.

Amplified reflection

When a client makes a strong all-or-nothing statement under pressure ("This is the worst thing that could happen"), repeat it back in an exaggerated form ("Yes, this is the absolute worst thing that could ever happen"). Hearing it amplified can help them rethink how true it is.

Prevent feedback friction

Feedback can feel critical depending on how either side delivers it. In the first session, agree how you each prefer to give and receive feedback. Keep paying attention to your language as challenges come up.

Aim of your reflections: encourage clarification, exploration and self-empathy, and open space to brainstorm re-engagement and renewed motivation.

Keep realistic expectations about what may come up in a coaching career, and be confident you have the tools to handle it.

Coaching in practice

  • "I've noticed the walks haven't happened the last couple of weeks. What's been going on?"
  • "It sounds like a lot has changed at home. How are you doing with all of that?"
  • "Nothing at all has worked, ever?" (amplified reflection, said gently)
  • "What would make this feel more doable right now?"
  • "How is our way of working together landing for you? Is there something I could do differently?"
  • "Last time we agreed you'd tell me if feedback felt too blunt. How did today feel?"
