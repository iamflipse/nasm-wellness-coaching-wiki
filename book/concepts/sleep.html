Sleep Hygiene and Optimizing Sleep

Lessons covered: 27 (Sleep), 28 (Optimizing Sleep)

Key ideas

  • Sleep is the "Swiss Army knife of health": whatever is going on, sleep makes it a little better.
  • Sleep is basic hygiene, like brushing your teeth or showering. Nobody boasts about skipping those because they're busy, so we shouldn't say it about sleep.
  • Sleep debt must be repaid; the lesson compares sleep to a balanced chequebook. Catching up on a Saturday restores some lost cognitive function, but the best habit is going to bed and waking at the same time every day. Chronic sleep restriction is linked with poor physical and mental health.
  • The course cites an annual US economic cost of insufficient sleep and recovery of over $400 billion, partly from more than a million lost working hours (Lesson 28).
  • Sleep is called by far the cheapest and most effective recovery and regeneration strategy (Lesson 28).

What happens during sleep

StageWhat the course says
N1Light sleep
N2Deeper sleep
N3 / slow-wave sleepThe deepest sleep. The brain clears toxic proteins such as beta-amyloid (associated with Alzheimer's); declarative memories form. Deep non-REM sleep replenishes energy stores (glucose, fats), repairs and grows tissue (especially skeletal muscle) and triggers recovery hormones
REMThe brain acts as if awake: lots of thoughts and emotions. About 70% of REM dreams are negative, which helps process yesterday's emotions and prepare for tomorrow's. Muscle atonia (paralysis at the spinal cord level) stops you acting out dreams. REM supports memory consolidation and brain health
  • Sleep consolidates motor, declarative and emotional memories.
  • Waking from REM is gentle. Waking up feeling "hit by a truck" suggests you woke from slow-wave sleep, and probably aren't getting enough sleep.

Signs of good sleep (Lesson 28)

  1. Falling asleep within 30 minutes
  2. Night awakenings are minimal and don't exceed 10 minutes
  3. No night sweats
  4. Waking refreshed and recalling or believing you have dreamed

Sleep hygiene strategies (Lesson 27)

StrategyWhy
Consistent bed and wake times, every dayThe hypothalamus regulates hormones that peak and dip at set times of day
Limit light at night, especially short-wavelength (blue) lightLight-sensitive retinal cells (intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells) signal the pineal gland; in darkness or dim light, melatonin is released
Cool room: about 65–70 °FThe optimal temperature for most people to start feeling sleepy
Address worryMany cases of insomnia are really anxiety. Treat the worry and sleep improves. CBT for insomnia is mentioned
More physical activityImmune factors released during exercise help us feel sleepy
Limit caffeine, especially in the afternoonCaffeine blocks adenosine (which builds sleep pressure) and has about a 6-hour half-life
Nap smartAround 3–4 p.m. cortisol drops and sleepiness rises. Nap 15 minutes (or up to 30, to avoid deep sleep) or a full 90-minute cycle, to avoid sleep inertia

Coaching in practice

  • "What time do you usually go to bed and get up, on weekdays and on weekends?"
  • "How do you feel when the alarm goes off?"
  • "What does the last hour before bed look like? Screens, light, temperature?"
  • "When do you have your last coffee?"
  • "When you can't sleep, what's going through your mind?"
  • "Which one change, a set wake time, a cooler room, earlier caffeine, feels doable this week?"
