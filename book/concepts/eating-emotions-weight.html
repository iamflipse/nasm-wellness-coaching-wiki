Eating, Emotions and Body Weight

Lessons covered: 41 (Eating & Emotional Wellbeing), 42 (Weight-Neutral Approach), 43 (Intuitive Eating)

Eating and emotional wellbeing (Lesson 41)

The social side of food

  • Long-term health effects of diet include food's psychological importance.
  • Mediterranean-diet research is cited: the social support, community, relaxation and enjoyment of sharing food stimulate enjoyment of healthy diets. Even when the food isn't "healthy", the social experience brings health benefits.
  • Eating together may have evolved to support social bonding.
  • Within a healthy overall pattern, a meal or a day of discretionary eating has more positive than negative effects.

Stress and eating

  • Stress can cause under-eating in some people and over-eating in others.
  • Acute and chronic stress can drive cravings for energy-dense, hyper-palatable foods for self-soothing.
  • Work, personal life and stress-management skills can be barriers to change.

Mindful eating

Paying attention to hunger, fullness, taste satisfaction, cravings, emotional eating and other triggers, with self-acceptance. Research cited suggests it may reduce visceral fat, cortisol and later over-snacking.

Dichotomous thinking and disinhibited eating

  • Dichotomous thinking means seeing foods as purely "good" or "bad".
  • It can lead to disinhibited eating: after "breaking a rule", the person overeats in response.
  • Teach that no single food is good or bad. The overall pattern matters most. Watch for inflexible food rules.

Reward-driven eating

  • The brain's reward system drives wanting food (dopamine) and enjoying it in the moment (opioids and cannabinoids).
  • "Food addiction" is a term applied to people more susceptible to a reward response to hyper-palatable foods. Signs to watch for: severe binges and cravings, high responsiveness to palatable foods, impulsivity, addictive personality traits.

The balanced position:

  • The two approaches aren't mutually exclusive. Take the positives of each as they fit the client's needs.
  • Body weight doesn't automatically determine health status, disease risk or quality of life. It's one factor among many.
  • Weight is not just a self-control problem: social, economic, environmental and biological factors all play a part.
  • The focus is overall wellbeing, not diet behaviours or weight categories. Help clients build confidence in long-term changes instead of chasing a number on a scale.
  • The body positivity movement has helped the conversation about weight bias, especially in health care.

Intuitive eating (Lesson 43)

Definition: eating based on physiological hunger and satiety cues rather than situational and emotional cues. It is directly linked with psychological wellbeing.

The 10 principles

#Principle
1Reject the diet mentality
2Honor your hunger
3Make peace with food
4Challenge the food police
5Discover the satisfaction factor
6Feel your fullness
7Cope with your emotions with kindness
8Respect your body
9Feel the difference in movement (as worded in the course)
10Honor your health with gentle nutrition

Four core features

  1. Unconditional permission to eat: eat when hungry, no guilt or shame, no "good" or "bad" foods.
  2. Eat for physical, not emotional, reasons: eat when hungry, stop when full; interrupt cycles of using food to cope with distress.
  3. Trust hunger and satiety cues: reject rigid external rules that ignore how you feel.
  4. Body–food choice congruence: food serves nutrition, taste, energy and enjoyment. Notice which foods give the most energy, fullness and nourishment.

Notes: intuitive eating opposes intentional weight loss through restriction and diet culture, though weight loss can still happen. Reported benefits include better body satisfaction and self-compassion; lower BMI, blood pressure, triglycerides and food preoccupation; and less anxiety and depression.

Mindful eating is a component: awareness of the taste, smell and texture of food, being present, eating without judgement. The course quotes the Center for Mindful Eating's definition of mindfulness as bringing full attention and awareness to the present moment without judgement. It seems most effective for binge eating, emotional eating and eating in response to external cues. Tools to offer clients: intuitive eating, mindful eating and the hunger scale.

Coaching in practice

  • "Before you eat, where would you put your hunger on a scale?"
  • "How do you feel after a meal like that, physically and emotionally?"
  • "When you're stressed, what happens with your eating?"
  • "What happens after you 'break a rule' with food?"
  • "Which meals with other people do you most enjoy?"
  • "Apart from the scale, what would tell you that you're getting healthier?"
  • "What would it be like to eat that meal slowly, noticing the taste and texture?"
