The Coach's Role, Evidence and the Coaching Relationship

Lessons covered: 4 (Coach's Primary Responsibility), 5 (Evidence-Based Practice), 6 (Coaching Relationships), 47–48 (The Power of Helping One Person), 49 (Effective Coaching)

The coach's primary responsibility (Lesson 4)

  • The coach's main job is to increase the client's awareness of what is influencing their health and life. That awareness helps them start thinking about change.
  • Whole-person health: it is not just diet, exercise or stress. Stress management, sleep hygiene, food choices and exercise are intertwined. Consistent choices across them create an upward spiral towards vitality, energy and resilience.
  • The aim is for clients to experience health, not just think about it. The lesson compares it to seeing a flower in bloom: you feel its beauty without having to name it.
  • Meeting clients where they are comes from curiosity. One expert calls it "imaginative empathy": a genuine wish to know what it is like to be this person.
  • Trust small steps. Ask for the smallest choice the person could make today that fits their goal, even if they can't yet see how it gets them there.

Evidence-based practice (Lesson 5)

  • Check the science behind claims. Build critical-thinking and research skills.
  • Peer-reviewed journals are the gold standard: other scientists review a paper, then accept it, ask for revisions or reject it.
  • Combine evidence with empathy to find the right balance for each person.
  • Work with humility and honesty, in partnership, helping clients find answers and workable solutions.

The coaching relationship (Lesson 6)

The relationship is described as the foundation of coaching success and the essential factor in outcomes. It is your responsibility to build trust and rapport.

FeatureWhat it looks like
The coaching containerA safe space where the client feels seen, heard and respected, and where action and change can happen
Client-centred and client-drivenThe client defines success. The coach never dictates how or what
Co-createdThe agenda, the plan and even each session emerge from collaboration, not top-down direction
Process and outcomeThe tools, mindset shifts and practices gained along the way matter as much as the end goal; the client keeps them for future goals
PresenceCuriosity, listening (verbal and non-verbal), witnessing, thoughtful questions and reflection
Not fixingThe coach is there to listen, not to fix the client

"It's all about you and it's not at all about you." This means being aware of yourself and of the client. Self-awareness stops your own projections, story or reactions from tripping you up. It is never the client's job to hold space for your emotional process.

Effective coaching (Lesson 49)

  • Rapport and communication build trust. Consistency and self-awareness are also key.
  • Intuition: trust yourself to choose the right questions, and let the client guide the conversation.
  • Empathy means understanding and sharing another person's feelings. It is not sympathy (feeling sorry for someone).
  • Active listening: take in what the client says fully, then ask permission before sharing what you know.
  • Reflective listening: paraphrase their words back to them. This brings clarity and shows that what they share matters.
  • Listen beyond words, use open-ended questions, and let silence feel comforting and safe.

The power of helping one person (Lessons 47–48)

These are short reflections. You never know how helping the one person in front of you might ripple out to many others. Lesson 48 gives an example of a client who came back after four weeks of making the agreed changes, feeling and moving better at home and at work.

Coaching in practice

  • "What's the smallest choice you could make today that fits this goal?"
  • "Can I share what I know about this, or would you be open to hearing what has worked for others?"
  • "What I hear you saying is… Did I get that right?"
  • "If I understand you correctly, you're experiencing…"
  • "What would you like to focus on in our time today?"
  • Allow a pause after a big statement instead of filling the silence.
