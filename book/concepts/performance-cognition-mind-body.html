Performance, Cognition and the Mind–Body Connection

Lessons covered: 24 (Performance & Cognition), 25 (Performance & Cognition Q&A), 26 (Mind–Body Connection)

Arousal and performance: the inverted U (Lesson 24)

Arousal levelWhat happensPerformance
Too lowSleepy, boredSuffers
Sweet spot ("flow")Epinephrine, cortisol and glucose rise a little; attention and thinking are sharpBest
Too highEpinephrine, cortisol and glucose very high; shakingSuffers

Why too much hurts: stress hormones bind to brain areas needed for performance, especially the hippocampus (which has cortisol receptors). The hippocampus is the learning and memory hub, and it also helps shut down the stress response. When it is saturated, you can't recall things in the moment and the stress response is harder to switch off.

Getting back to the sweet spot: reframe or reappraise the situation, or use stress-reduction tools such as deep breathing.

Cognitive load and attention

  • Cognitive resources are limited. Divide attention and every task suffers.
  • Attention is our most precious resource: the most costly thing for the brain to do, and something it doesn't like doing. People spend about half their lives mind-wandering.
  • Learning to point the attentional spotlight at the task at hand is a big advantage.
  • Mind and body are linked: when cognitive load rises, musculoskeletal load rises too. Muscles tense without our awareness. On busy days, suggest short breaks for progressive relaxation or deep breathing.

Mindset, cognition and emotion

  • Fixed vs growth mindset affects learning and performance; mindsets can change (see Growth Mindset).
  • Emotion always influences cognition, and cognition can help regulate emotion (down-regulating unhelpful emotions).
StrategyWhat it isEffectiveness (per the course)
Cognitive reappraisalMentally changing how you interpret an emotional event to lessen its impactUsually effective
SuppressionPushing the emotion downUsually not effective

When the frontal lobe is flooded with stress hormones it doesn't work well, which makes self-control harder.

Mindfulness and the reward system (Lesson 25)

  • Mindfulness (awareness of where your attention is) is among the best things for cognitive health. Remembering a happy moment shows you were present then.
  • The expert admits being a chronic mind-wanderer: mindfulness is a practice, not perfection.
  • The brain is hardwired for survival drives. The hypothalamus drives the "four Fs" (fight, flight, food and sex) with or without our permission.
  • Survival-related behaviours are rewarded with dopamine via the mesolimbic dopaminergic pathway: ventral tegmental area → nucleus accumbens → prefrontal cortex. Carbohydrate-rich treats may also release serotonin.
  • Our ancestors survived by preferring high-fat, high-calorie food. With unlimited access today, we sometimes need to rein that in.

Embodied cognition (Lesson 26)

ViewIdea
Traditional cognitive viewThe body sends sensory input to the brain; the brain sends motor output
Embodied cognitionThe whole body influences cognition: it thinks, processes and produces behaviour in its own way
  • Example: walking comes largely from how the body is built. The brain may handle navigation, but the body adjusts pace and balance automatically.
  • Emotions are felt in the whole body ("a punch in the gut", "a slap in the face").
  • For coaching: consider how a client's physical and mental worlds work together to carry out a wellness behaviour, and help them feel physically and mentally connected.

Coaching in practice

  • "When you're performing at your best, how keyed-up do you feel? Too flat, just right, or too wired?"
  • "Before that presentation, what could bring you from 'shaking' back to 'sharp'?"
  • "On your busiest days, where do you notice tension in your body?"
  • "How many things are you trying to hold in your head at once? What can come off the list?"
  • "Is there another way to look at what happened?" (cognitive reappraisal)
  • "Where do you feel that emotion in your body?"
