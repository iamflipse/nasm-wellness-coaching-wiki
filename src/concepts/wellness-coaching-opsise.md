# Wellness Coaching and the Six Dimensions (OPSISE)

*Lessons covered: 1 (Welcome), 3 (Dimensions of Wellness)*

## Key ideas

- **NASM's definition:** wellness coaching is the active process of applying learned and developed strategies that lead to the mental, emotional and physical betterment of life.
- **It all comes down to choices.** The coach doesn't dictate. The coach supports clients in making their own choices and finding their own best practices.
- **Not perfection.** The goal is not to be perfect in any one area. The areas of wellness work together to improve stress levels, health and peace of mind.
- **The end goal is independence.** The coach guides, motivates and encourages so that clients form habits they can eventually keep up on their own (Lesson 1).
- **Mind–body link.** Most body systems (gut, immune system, heart, muscles, even bones) communicate in both directions with the brain, so brain wellness is part of whole-body wellness (Lesson 1).

### Balance and consistency

| Term | Meaning in the course |
|---|---|
| **Balance** | Helping clients find stability between their stressors and their ability to cope, and equilibrium across the areas of wellness |
| **Consistency** | Making small adjustments to behaviour that add up to significant long-term change |

## The six dimensions of wellness (OPSISE)

| Letter | Dimension | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **O** | Occupational | A client's work and their attitude towards it. Contentment depends on the career fitting their interests, beliefs and values. |
| **P** | Physical | Nutrition, hydration and physical activity. Regular movement is linked with higher levels in other dimensions, such as mental and emotional wellness. |
| **S** | Social | Healthy relationships, which can lead to a healthier lifestyle. Interaction is described as a necessity for human existence. |
| **I** | Intellectual | Stimulating the mind. People who challenge their beliefs, think creatively, solve problems and plan report less stress and conflict. |
| **S** | Spiritual | Aligning actions with values and beliefs; questioning one's own existence and being tolerant of other beliefs. |
| **E** | Emotional | Identifying and accepting feelings. Clients who express feelings freely build deeper relationships and handle conflict with more clarity and maturity. |

### The coach's scope

Occupational, spiritual and intellectual wellness matter, but the course says they **fall outside a wellness coach's influence**. An NASM wellness coach focuses on **physical** and **mental/emotional** wellness through four focus areas:

1. Movement
2. Mental and emotional wellbeing
3. Nutrition
4. Recovery and regeneration

(The welcome lesson lists the course's five areas as these four plus **coaching** itself.)

## Ambivalence and small steps

- **Ambivalence** means having conflicting desires. Coaches often have to help clients through it.
- In moments of ambivalence, remind clients that **small changes can produce lasting results**.
- When motivated, people tend to do **too much too soon**: throwing out every treat in the house, or running three miles after a year of nothing more than short walks. These surges rarely last and can knock other areas, such as mental wellbeing and recovery, off track.
- **Celebrate micro-victories.** Small wins create upward spirals and positive feedback loops that strengthen autonomy, meaning and purpose, and connection with others.
- Motivational interviewing helps here: listen without judgement or correction to understand the client's perspective and meet them where they are.

## Coaching in practice

- "Which area of your wellness feels most important to you right now?"
- "What's one small, consistent change you could make this week?"
- "Part of you wants this change and part of you doesn't. Tell me about both sides."
- "You did that three days in a row. How does it feel to notice that?"
- When a client wants to overhaul everything at once: "That's a lot of energy. What's the smallest piece of this we could start with so it lasts?"
