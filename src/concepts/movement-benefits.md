# Benefits of Movement

*Lessons covered: 12 (Physical Activity & Wellness), 13 (Muscle), 14 (Sedentary Behaviors), 15 (Movement: Mental & Emotional)*

## Everyday movement counts (Lesson 12)

- Regular movement through the day is a **cornerstone of any wellness strategy**: "use it or lose it".
- **Activities of daily living count**: household chores, standing during TV adverts, pacing while on the phone.
- Benefits can start **before any structured exercise**: better sleep, immune function, and heart and metabolic function.
- Simply shifting a client's **relationship with movement** and their awareness of its benefits can contribute to positive physiological change.
- **The big practical finding:** interrupting prolonged sitting with **brief light-to-moderate movement about every 30 minutes** has been shown to improve a range of physiological functions. No gym, equipment or heavy weights needed.

### Risks of too little movement (as listed in the course)

| Population | Associated risks |
|---|---|
| **Adults** | Type 2 diabetes; cardiovascular disease incidence and mortality; moderate relative risk for ovarian, colon and endometrial cancers; more depression, anxiety and stress; lower cognitive function |
| **Young people** | Strong evidence for obesity; moderate evidence for hypertension and raised total cholesterol; low self-esteem, social and behavioural problems, lower fitness and academic achievement |

Screen time outside work and school is noted as an added concern.

## Muscle (Lesson 13)

- Muscle is called **"the currency of aging"** and described as the body's **biggest endocrine organ**: it makes hormones and responds to hormonal signals.
- The goal is not just building muscle but **maintaining lean muscle mass** with age, for resilience, work capacity and quality of life.

## Sedentary behaviour (Lesson 14)

- "Sitting is the new smoking"? The expert says sitting itself isn't, but **sedentary behaviours probably are**: epidemiological data show a pattern similar to smoking.
- **Sedentary** = seated, reclining or lying down. People who sit more have a higher **relative risk** of heart disease, cancer and metabolic disease (metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes).
- **Exercisers can still be sedentary.** If someone spends most of the day in those positions, exercise **does not undo** it.
- **Takeaway:** not "exercise more", but **get up, walk around, sit and recline less**. Break up sitting with standing or walking.
- Work out with clients **what they believe they can do**. When it's collaborative, they're far more likely to stick with it.

## Mental and emotional benefits (Lesson 15)

Moving for **wellness** focuses on feelings, enjoyment and mood. Moving for **fitness** uses similar methods with a different aim.

| Benefit | What the course says |
|---|---|
| **Stress tolerance** | Stress is a perceived threat that disrupts homeostasis. **Eustress** (positive challenge) drives growth; **distress** (feeling overwhelmed) hurts performance, health and longevity. Exercise lowers neurological sensitivity to stress. Movement uses up blood sugar and stress hormones and normalises heart rate and blood pressure. When active people become inactive, they report worse mood and more irritability. |
| **Vitality** | **Subjective vitality** is the perceived feeling of having enough energy. Someone can look healthy but feel low in vitality. Movement helps through circulation, metabolism and sleep. |
| **Mood and emotion** | Positive emotions rise right after activity and can last up to **24 hours**. Exercise increases sensitivity to **dopamine and serotonin** and affects the **endocannabinoid and opioid** systems. Enjoyable movement gets repeated. |
| **Social connection** | Moving with others means more activity, more enjoyment and better adherence. It builds belonging and trust and reduces loneliness; especially useful for isolated clients. |
| **Mental health** | Movement improves mood and reduces depression and anxiety. Some clients may struggle to self-start. |
| **Cognition and productivity** | Focus, information processing, creative problem solving and productivity all improve. Aerobic and strength activity are both linked to benefits. |
| **Brain health** | **Neuroplasticity** (the brain adapting, learning, remembering) is supported. Resistance and aerobic training **at least twice a week** can raise processing speed, **BDNF** (which builds new brain connections) and total brain volume. There are protective effects against neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's. Rhythmic (dance), novel and higher-intensity movement add further benefit. |

**Autonomy tip from Lesson 15:** offer choices, and steer towards activities the client feels **mastery** in, can **relate** to, and can do while **connecting** with others.

## Coaching in practice

- "Walk me through a typical day. Where are you already moving?"
- "Do you count things like housework or walking the dog? They count."
- "How could you break up long stretches of sitting? Every 30 minutes, what could you do?"
- "On a scale of 1 to 10, how certain are you that you could do that?"
- "Could any meetings or phone calls happen on your feet?"
- "How do you feel for the rest of the day after you move?"
