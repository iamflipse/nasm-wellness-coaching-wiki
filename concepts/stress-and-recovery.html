Stress Management and Recovery

Lessons covered: 21 (Stress Management), 22 (Stress Q&A), 23 (Stress & Recovery), 32 (Stress Reduction Strategies)

What stress is

  • Stress is what happens when the body or mind meets a demanding situation or stimulus. Emotional stress can change brain and hormonal activity (Lesson 32).
  • Sources can be external (finances, work, relationship changes) or internal (illness, fear, poor nutrition).
  • The fight-or-flight response is an evolved, primal reaction, well adapted to acute threats to life and safety (Lesson 21).
  • Our brains are wired to notice negatives (see Negativity Bias). That kept ancestors alive, but biology hasn't kept up with technology.
  • The real problem for modern people is chronic, perceived emotional stress, especially when it is unpredictable and uncontrollable. We are also very good at ruminating on it.
  • Because most modern stress is psychological and perceived, we have more control over it than we might think.

Allostatic load (Lesson 32)

Stressors that chronically activate the stress response create allostatic load: the cumulative wear and tear of stress on the body. A heavy load can erode muscles, bones, organs and brain tissue. Chronic stress, even at low daily levels, is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease, autoimmune disorders, anxiety and depression. Returning to homeostasis and managing allostatic load are essential to living well.

Not all stress is bad

  • Eustress (positive stress) drives growth and sometimes needs to increase to improve health and fitness. A hard workout stresses the body but may destress the mind (Lesson 23).
  • Some pressure improves performance. Skill, personality, confidence and task complexity all affect how each person handles it (Lesson 32).

The stress management toolbox (Lesson 21)

ToolHow it works
Deep, slow breathingShifts the body from the sympathetic (fight-or-flight) to the parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) system. It improves with practice
ReframingReinterpret body signals; for example, before a speech read the jitters as excitement, not nervousness
Meditation / mindfulnessMany types; all quiet the mind and focus attention on the present. We spend about half our lives not paying attention, and happy moments are moments when we were paying attention
Good sleep hygieneSleep processes yesterday's emotional events and prepares us for tomorrow's. Stress, depression and poor sleep are bidirectionally linked
Small amounts of movementPark further away, take the stairs. Exercise releases signals such as osteocalcin (from bone) and BDNF, which the expert calls "fertiliser for the brain"
Emotional granularityBeing able to tell emotions apart lets you choose specific, measured responses
Hugs (with permission)Meaningful hugs may reduce fear and anxiety circuitry and release oxytocin: social stress buffering
Managing thoughtsMost stress is daily hassles plus our thinking about them. Learning to stop ruminating is hard and takes practice, but it is a skill

Other coping activities (Lesson 32): grounding, reading, music (helps return the body to homeostasis), journaling, gratitude practice and hobbies.

Emotional pain is real pain (Lesson 22)

  • Language across cultures describes emotional hurt as pain. The brain largely uses the same circuitry for physical and emotional pain.
  • In lab conditions, acetaminophen also reduced emotional pain responses, which the expert offers as evidence that emotional pain is real.
  • Be gentle with each other and with yourself.
  • Gratitude and compassion meditation have short-term mood effects and, with practice, may change the brain towards being "happier at rest" through neuroplasticity.
  • Vagal tone (related to the vagus nerve, the 10th cranial nerve, which reaches most major organs) is described as a measure of stress, and there is an upward spiral between positive emotion and vagal tone.

Recovery and regeneration (Lesson 23)

  • Mindfulness, quality sleep, time with friends and family, and healthy dietary patterns reduce stress, improve wellbeing and restore readiness for new stressors.
  • Recovery cannot happen without stress; without stress there is nothing to recover from.
  • Key recovery pillars: sleep, nutrition and quiet wakefulness.
  • Life is messy. The aim is not to remove all stress but to balance stress and stress relief for good health, wellness and performance.

Coaching in practice

  • "When you notice stress building, what happens in your body?"
  • "Can we try three slow breaths together right now?"
  • "What's another way to read those butterflies? Could they be excitement?"
  • "Think of a recent happy moment, even a small one. Where was your attention?"
  • "How much of this is happening now, and how much is replaying or worrying ahead?"
  • "Which of these, breathing, music, a walk, journaling, would you be willing to try this week?"
  • "What does recovery look like in your week? Where is your quiet time?"
