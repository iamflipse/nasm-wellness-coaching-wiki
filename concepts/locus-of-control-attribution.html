Locus of Control and Attributional Retraining

Lessons covered: 7 (Locus of Control), 8 (Attribution Retraining / Blame)

Locus of control (Lesson 7)

Locus of control is how much control a person feels they have over parts of their life, such as their health and wellness. It affects behaviour, confidence and consistency.

Internal locus of controlExternal locus of control
Belief"I have direct control over what happens to me""My fate is up to others or to chance"
SkillsBelieves they have the knowledge and skills to overcome challengesDoesn't believe they can change the situation
Typical responseTakes ownershipBlames outside sources and feels helpless
Health linkDescribed as a good thing for wellnessLinked with higher stress and more illness

What shapes it

The course names three factors:

  1. How much control the person perceives they have
  2. Their ability to adapt and modify behaviours
  3. Their capacity to predict outcomes from specific behaviours

Building an internal locus

  • Awareness and education are key.
  • Help clients identify the behaviours they can control, and build a plan around their strengths.
  • Reprioritise behaviours so they support the client's goals.
  • Learn how and when to modify behaviours. When target behaviours change, long-term goals may need to change too.
  • Help clients predict likely outcomes of their behaviours.
  • Autonomy and empowerment along the way increase their sense of control, and lead towards self-efficacy.

Attribution and attributional retraining (Lesson 8)

Clients will often say things are getting in the way, and the lesson says these are almost always external: time, work, family. Attribution theory asks what is getting in the way (what the person blames). Attributional retraining asks how can I retrain my thoughts, concepts and perceptions to get past it.

Three questions about an attribution

QuestionOptions
Where is it?Internal or external
Can it be controlled?Controllable or uncontrollable
Will it last?Stable or unstable

The worst place for a client is believing the obstacle is uncontrollable and will never change.

How to retrain

  • Go small. Big change needs a lot of motivation, and nobody is highly motivated all the time. Pick a step small enough that it still works when motivation is low.
  • It doesn't have to be challenging. It has to be something the client can actually do.
  • Celebrate it, then do it again.
  • Drop the all-or-nothing mentality. Do a little bit and let behaviour change build along the way.
  • Two ingredients: the right strategy, and actual effort (even at small levels).
  • Small strategies let clients get around blame and guilt, and build confidence bit by bit until they can move forward on their own.

Coaching in practice

  • "What gets in the way most often?" Then explore it: "Is that something within your control, partly in your control, or outside it?"
  • "Is that always true, or are there times it's different?" (tests whether the obstacle is stable)
  • "Given that your schedule is packed, what's a version of this so small you could do it even on your worst day?"
  • "Which parts of your day do you feel most in charge of?"
  • "If you did X, what do you predict would happen?"
  • "You did it. Let's take a moment to celebrate that."

Quotes

"Big change takes a lot of motivation, and you're not going to be a lot motivated all the time." (Lesson 8)

"This isn't about all or nothing. It's about doing a little bit." (Lesson 8)
