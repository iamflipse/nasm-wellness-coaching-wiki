Negativity Bias, Self-Compassion and Shame

Lessons covered: 9 (Negativity Bias / Cutting Yourself Slack), 54 (Addressing Shame with Empathy), 55 (Addressing Shame Q&A)

Negativity bias (Lesson 9)

  • All humans struggle and suffer. Breaking the cycle of beating yourself up is what the lesson calls "cutting yourself some slack".
  • Negativity bias is a tendency to focus more on the challenging or negative things around us than on the positive ones.
  • It helped our ancestors survive, because watching for threats kept them and their dependants alive.
  • Today, with basic needs mostly met, it can mean dwelling on everything that is wrong. That predisposes people to anxiety, depression and negative self-talk.
  • Knowing the bias exists lets us get ahead of it by deliberately focusing on the positives in ourselves and the world.

(The bias also appears in Stress and Social Wellness, where the course describes us as an ancient brain in a modern environment.)

Self-compassion: three parts (Lesson 9)

PartWhat it isIts opposite
Common humanityRecognising that everyone suffers and struggles, and that you deserve support and care like anyone elseIsolating, withdrawing, telling yourself you're broken
Self-kindnessBeing warm, patient and comforting about your own shortcomingsIgnoring, belittling or criticising yourself
MindfulnessExamining your flaws openly and without judgement, neither over-identifying ("I'm a loser") nor suppressing ("suck it up")Getting lost in the negative story

Practical tools from the lesson:

  • Treat yourself as you would treat a dear friend.
  • Comforting touch: safe touch soothes the nervous system (a massage, a hug, or a weighted blanket if no one is around).
  • Mindfulness practices: meditation, mindful eating or walking, journaling.
  • Treat emotions as information: "What are my feelings trying to tell me?" Meeting reactions with curiosity over criticism breaks the spiral of self-judgement and builds emotional intelligence.
  • Practising on yourself makes you better at meeting clients' struggles with compassion.

Shame (Lesson 54)

  • The lesson quotes a researcher's definition (the name is cut off in the transcript): shame is an intensely painful feeling of believing we are flawed and therefore unworthy of love and belonging.
  • Three primary factors around shame: feeling trapped, powerless and isolated.
  • It can show up in any wellness area: nutrition, movement, emotional wellbeing.

How shame shows up in sessions

  • Distress at not being "allowed" to have faults, mess up, or even want change
  • Feeling unworthy of help or attention, or worrying they are boring the coach by repeating themselves
  • Limiting beliefs
  • Self-deprecating comments wrapped in humour
  • Anger, overwhelm, frustration, turning inward or shutting down

Shame lowers belief in the capacity to change and erodes trust in oneself, the coach and the process.

Empathy as the remedy

The expert calls empathy the "kryptonite" to shame: putting yourself in someone's shoes and caring about their experience.

  • Acknowledge it. When a client names shame, they have given you a gift of vulnerability. A simple affirmation that they've been heard can be healing.
  • Follow their lead and give them agency, while holding the container.
  • Don't rush. Their goals can be recognised without pushing towards them.
  • Coaching practices that help: engaged listening; respecting vulnerability; reinforcing worthiness, belonging and capacity; helping them notice and name when and why shame shows up; exploring social and cultural influences; asking what is on the other side of shame; asking whether shame is serving them; anchoring back to their strengths and values.
  • Shame resilience theory includes a relational element: being seen in your vulnerability and receiving empathy. Over time clients start coaching themselves.
  • Holding the client capable and able (generative accountability) also works well with shame. See Coaching Mindset.

Self-compassion is not self-indulgence (Lesson 55)

  • Make the self-criticism or shame the object of compassion. Most people find compassion for others easier than for themselves.
  • Shame or self-criticism is a sign that you care. Instead of caring less to escape the discomfort, double down on what matters.
  • Write down the words you would say to someone you care about, then say them to yourself.
  • Erase the slate: look for the very next action that fits the goal or value, and start again.
  • Compassion means seeing pain, wanting to help, and having the courage and discernment to respond in a way that actually helps. It is not reaching for a bad habit to make the feeling go away.

Coaching in practice

  • "If a close friend told you this, what would you say to them?"
  • "Thank you for sharing that with me. I know that isn't easy to say."
  • "It sounds like this matters a lot to you. That feeling might be a sign of how much you care."
  • "What's the very next small thing you could do that reflects this goal?"
  • "Is that voice helping you right now? Where do you think it comes from?"
  • "What strengths have carried you through hard times before?"
