Wellness Mindset: "Get To" vs "Have To"

Lessons covered: 70 (Wellness Mindset). Lesson 53 is titled "Get to vs Have to", but its transcript is about meaning in movement; see Movement Motivation.

Key ideas

  • Wellness is about getting to do things that feel good and make you feel good about yourself, your community and life. It is not about forcing yourself to do things that are good for you.
  • It is a process of self-discovery: following what feels good and right, and finding what helps you thrive.
  • What sets this apart from many health and fitness approaches: it is not about being told what you have to do, and not about shortcuts.
  • It stands for freedom and choice, hope and possibility, learning to trust and care for yourself.
  • The coach's goal is to help clients discover what creates health, happiness and meaning for them, not to get them to do what someone else has decided is good for them.

The mindset shift

Have-to mindsetGet-to (wellness) mindset
"I have to""I get to"
"I should""I want to"
"I will resent having to do this""I will be grateful for this"
Self-controlSelf-care

Why it matters:

  • Clients feel less resistance to trying new things and making changes.
  • After a setback there is less shame, so they are less likely to withdraw from their goals or from coaching.
  • Their whole wellness journey feels more positive and meaningful.

From vision to choice points

  1. Build a personal wellness vision. Lift the conversation above a number on a scale or a vague wish to "feel better". (Questions below.)
  2. Apply the vision to each domain. Ask clients to describe their ideal relationship with a domain such as food, movement or sleep.
  3. Find choice points. Pick an existing routine or time of day and ask what would make it better or more consistent with their vision.
  4. Co-create a small commitment they can try out to see whether it supports them. Share research-supported ideas, but encourage their creativity. Don't hand over a generic fix-it list.

The empowering coach–client relationship

  • Be a guide more than an expert: help clients find their own direction and then navigate it.
  • It starts with respect, empathy and curiosity. Listen and learn from what they share.
  • Balance evidence-based suggestions with encouraging clients to trust their own direct experience. You won't always know what is best, and the client is in charge of their wellness.
  • Root it in positive social connection: see the good in them, commiserate and celebrate, and hold hope when they are frustrated or unsure.
  • Each contact should leave them feeling understood, encouraged and more confident.

Your own mindset is contagious

The lesson opens by asking what you do for your own wellness that you enjoy and are grateful for. Mindsets are contagious, so a coach needs to exude hope, gratitude and self-trust. Keep reflecting on your own wellness journey. Role-modelling the mindset is a source of both empathy and inspiration.

Coaching in practice

Questions from lesson 70 for building a wellness vision:

  • "What brought you here? What is your definition of wellness?"
  • "What would wellness feel like? What would be different in your life?"
  • "What's holding you back: habits, beliefs, struggles? What are you ready to be free from?"
  • "What would wellness be in service of? Why do you want more energy or less stress?"
  • "What roles, relationships and goals matter most to you?"
  • "Describe your ideal relationship with food (or movement, or sleep)."
  • "Think about your mornings. What could make them a little more like that vision?"
  • Reframe: "What if this isn't something you have to do, but something you get to do?"
