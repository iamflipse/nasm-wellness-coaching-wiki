Healthy Dietary Patterns: Food First

Lessons covered: 34 (Healthy Diet), 35 (Cooking), 36 (Food vs Nutrients), 37 (Dietary Patterns), 38 (Q&A: Autophagy), 39 (Supporting Healthy Eating), 40 (Client Questions)

Rethinking "healthy diet" (Lesson 34)

  • "Healthy" and "diet" together often cause conflicting feelings. Wellness is holistic and shouldn't include stress or shame about food.
  • A diet built on exact calories, macros and micros is extremely restrictive. It can cause stress, preoccupation with food, cutting out favourite foods, and skipping social occasions.
  • A truly healthy diet considers factors beyond nutritional value: lifestyle, social circle, culture, emotional state.
  • Dietary pattern (the definition quoted in the course): the totality of what someone habitually eats and drinks, with the parts acting synergistically on health.
  • Goal: an overall pattern that, on average, has a net positive effect on health, rather than fixating on single nutrients, foods or meals.
  • Positive psychological outcomes come from enjoying taste, being included in social food gatherings, and eating in line with cultural and moral values without guilt or shame.

Food vs nutrients (Lesson 36)

  • Nutrition is the process of taking in food and using its energy and nutrients for growth and repair: ingestion, digestion, absorption, transportation, assimilation and excretion.
  • There is no single optimal, universal human diet. Humans adapted to every environment; diets are shaped by region, climate, culture, religion, food availability, socioeconomics and preference.
  • Healthy patterns share traits:
Lower inHigher in
Processed foodsFibre
Animal fatFruits and vegetables
Refined grainsSeafood, dairy
Added sugarsNuts and legumes
  • Why food first: patterns allow many routes to a healthy diet and translate easily into eating behaviours, which are practical and easier to implement than nutrient targets.

Why single-nutrient thinking falls short

EraDisease typeExample
Early 20th centurySingle-nutrient deficiency: develops quickly, reverses quickly when the nutrient returnsVitamin D → rickets; iodine → goitre
Late 1940s onwards (heart disease); 1970s onwards (obesity, type 2 diabetes)Chronic disease: develops over time with many causes beyond dietCoronary heart disease

Saturated fat example: high saturated fat raises heart-disease risk, but the strength of that link depends on the rest of the diet. Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat gives the biggest risk reduction. Replacing it with refined carbs and added sugar gives no change. What replaces a nutrient matters.

Flexibility: the 80–90% idea (Lesson 37)

  • A healthy pattern means flexibility, not rigid rules ("I can only eat salmon, I can only eat salad"), which tend to break people.
  • Expand variety: more vegetables, different legumes, suitable grains, varied proteins.
  • Focus on what you do 80–90% of the time, and allow room for social eating and holidays.
  • Programs must work for where people are: their culture, city and neighbourhood.
  • When people drift, use empathy and nudge them back to the pattern.

Night-time repair and autophagy (Lesson 38, Q&A)

  • For most people, night is repair time: processing the day's nutrients, rebuilding and rejuvenating. Hormones follow a circadian rhythm.
  • Autophagy is described as the body's way of cleaning up waste products of metabolism, "carrying out the trash" at the cellular level.
  • Acute inflammation after a workout is normal and switches off. Overtraining, overeating, stress and poor sleep push towards chronic inflammation.
  • Constant snacking and late eating may disrupt the shift into repair and clean-up.
  • The expert suggests a roughly 12-hour eating window (breakfast, lunch, dinner), perhaps 14 hours fasting and 10 eating, occasionally 16 hours, for digestive rest.
  • The expert also flags fibre as something modern diets lose.

Cooking as a wellness behaviour (Lesson 35)

  • In the US, home food preparation fell 23% between 1965 and 2007, while eating away from home rose 42%.
  • Eating more home-prepared food is linked to lower risk of lifestyle diseases (type 2 diabetes, heart disease).
  • Learning to cook is associated with lower anxiety and depression, higher self-rated happiness and more resilience.
  • Cooking can bring connection, positive emotion, accomplishment, purpose and self-nourishment. Culinary medicine (Harvard is cited) teaches patients cooking and nutrition.
  • Invite clients to consider how food gets from the ground to their body, and what values (local sourcing, climate, culture, tradition) they could express through food.

The coach's role in eating (Lessons 39–40)

  • Eating well is challenging and is the basis for wellness. Be supportive, empathetic and guiding.
  • Be the "strong tethering pole" or anchor when clients fall off the wagon.
  • Aim for consistency and a long health span, not a quick fix or temporary diet.
  • Use science to help clients cut through the noise and focus on what matters.

Coaching in practice

  • "Tell me about what a typical day of eating looks like for you."
  • "What foods do you love? Which ones connect you to your culture or family?"
  • "What would it look like if 80–90% of your meals supported how you want to feel?"
  • "What's one new vegetable or legume you'd be curious to try?"
  • "How do you feel about cooking? What's one simple meal you'd like to learn?"
  • "When do you usually stop eating in the evening?"
